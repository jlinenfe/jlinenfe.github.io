Everglades Wading Bird Foraging & Nesting Trends

Project Overview

In this project, I analyzed more than 30 years of nesting data to investigate long-term trends in wood stork and roseate spoonbill populations across the Greater Everglades Ecosystem.

Working with datasets collected by Everglades National Park, the South Florida Water Management District, Audubon Florida, and the University of Florida, I compiled and cleaned information from multiple sources to evaluate how nesting abundance, colony locations, and habitat use have changed through time.

Using statistical and spatial analysis in R and GIS, I examined how hydrologic conditions and restoration may influence wading bird nesting and foraging patterns across the Everglades.

Background

Roseate spoonbills and wood storks historically nested along the coastlines of Everglades National Park and Florida Bay, where seasonal changes in water level created predictable shallow-water foraging habitat.

Alteration of freshwater flows across the Everglades changed the timing, depth, and distribution of water throughout the ecosystem. These changes contributed to shifts in where wading birds forage and establish nesting colonies.

Large-scale Everglades restoration is intended to reestablish more natural freshwater flow and hydrologic patterns. Because wading birds respond strongly to hydrologic conditions and prey availability, their nesting and foraging patterns provide an important ecological indicator of how the ecosystem is responding.

Questions

The analysis was organized around several related ecological questions:

  • How have wood stork and roseate spoonbill nesting abundance and colony locations changed over the past 30 years?
  • How has wading bird foraging distribution changed across the Greater Everglades?
  • What hydrologic conditions are associated with successful coastal wood stork nesting?
  • Are restoration-related hydrologic changes supporting a return toward historic coastal habitat use?

Analytical Approach

Long-term nesting and foraging datasets collected by multiple agencies were compiled, standardized, and analyzed in R. Spatial information was also integrated using GIS to evaluate changes in colony and foraging distributions across the Greater Everglades.

Nesting abundance and locations were compared through time for wood storks and roseate spoonbills. Hydrologic information was then incorporated to examine relationships between environmental conditions and nesting response.

Particular attention was given to water depth and the seasonal recession of water levels because wading birds depend on the concentration of aquatic prey into accessible shallow-water habitats during the nesting season.

1. How Have Nesting Counts and Locations Changed?

Long-term nesting records show that wading bird abundance and colony locations have shifted substantially over the past 30 years.

Wood storks have increasingly returned to historic coastal colonies, providing evidence that improving hydrologic conditions may be supporting renewed use of these areas.

Roseate spoonbills, however, have shown a different spatial response, with nesting activity shifting farther inland.

This difference is important because it may not simply represent restoration failure. Rising sea levels can increase water depths in traditional Florida Bay foraging areas, potentially creating a new ecological baseline in which some historically suitable habitat is becoming less accessible.

2. How Does Water Depth Relate to Wood Stork Nesting?

Hydrology is one of the major controls on wading bird foraging habitat. Water must be deep enough to support aquatic prey production but shallow enough during the nesting season for birds to successfully capture that prey.

Correlation analysis examining coastal water depth and wood stork nest counts identified a relationship between annual hydrologic conditions and nesting response.

In the analyzed record, years with annual median coastal water depths greater than approximately 18 cm were associated with above-average wood stork nesting.

This provides a preliminary quantitative indicator of the hydrologic conditions that may support successful coastal nesting.

3. How Does Foraging Habitat Change Through the Nesting Season?

Wading bird nesting success depends on more than conditions immediately surrounding a colony. Birds require suitable foraging habitat across the broader Everglades landscape throughout the nesting season.

The spatial animation illustrates wood stork habitat use and foraging locations across the Everglades as hydrologic conditions change through time.

Successful conditions generally require sufficiently high water before the nesting season followed by a gradual recession. As water levels decline, aquatic prey become concentrated into progressively smaller wetland areas where they become accessible to foraging birds.

This landscape-scale progression helps sustain food availability from pre-nesting foraging through chick development and fledging.

Ecological Interpretation

The results illustrate why wading birds are useful indicators of Everglades restoration. Their response integrates conditions occurring across both space and time: sufficient wet-season water is needed to support prey production, while appropriate dry-season recession is needed to concentrate those prey into accessible foraging habitat.

The return of wood storks to historic coastal nesting areas provides an encouraging indication of ecological response. At the same time, the inland movement of roseate spoonbills illustrates that restoration is occurring within an ecosystem that is also being altered by sea-level rise and other long-term environmental changes.

Conclusion

More than three decades of monitoring reveal that wading bird nesting and foraging patterns across the Greater Everglades are not static. Both abundance and spatial distributions have changed as hydrologic conditions, restoration, and the surrounding environment have evolved.

Combining long-term biological monitoring with hydrologic and spatial analysis provides a way to move beyond simply counting nests and instead evaluate why, where, and under what environmental conditions wading birds are responding.

Continued monitoring will be important for distinguishing restoration-driven recovery from longer-term shifts in habitat suitability and for informing future Everglades water-management decisions.
