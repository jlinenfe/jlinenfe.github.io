Global Climate Change Analysis

Project Overview

Climate change is often discussed through individual indicators: atmospheric carbon dioxide, global temperature, melting ice, or rising sea level. This project was developed to examine these indicators together and evaluate how the major components of the modern climate record have changed through time.

Using publicly available observational datasets, I analyzed long-term carbon dioxide emissions, global temperature anomalies, global sea level, and satellite-era measurements of mass loss from the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets.

Rather than treating each dataset as an isolated trend, the analysis follows the progression of the climate system from increasing greenhouse-gas emissions to warming, loss of land-based ice, and rising global sea level.

Questions

The analysis was organized around several related questions:

  • How have global carbon dioxide emissions changed since the beginning of the modern industrial era?
  • How has Earth's average surface temperature changed over the same period?
  • Has global sea level changed alongside the observed warming?
  • How much ice are Greenland and Antarctica currently losing?
  • Has the rate of ice-sheet loss changed through time?
  • How much measurable sea-level rise has recent ice-sheet loss contributed to the world's oceans?

Analytical Approach

Multiple datasets covering different components of Earth's climate system were cleaned, organized, analyzed, and visualized in R. Long-term records beginning near 1880 were first used to examine changes in global carbon dioxide emissions, temperature, and sea level.

The analysis was then narrowed to the satellite era to investigate changes in the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets using IMBIE mass balance estimates from 1992–2020. Annual ice loss was compared through time and converted to sea-level equivalent to quantify how much water these losses added to the global ocean.

1. How Have Global CO₂ Emissions Changed?

The first part of the analysis examined the human driver underlying modern climate change: carbon dioxide emissions from fossil-fuel use and industrial activity.

Global emissions increased substantially following industrialization, with the most rapid growth occurring after the middle of the twentieth century. The long-term record shows that annual emissions increased by roughly 34,300 million tonnes between 1880 and the modern period represented in the dataset.

Carbon dioxide is a greenhouse gas. Increasing its concentration in the atmosphere reduces the amount of outgoing heat that escapes to space, altering Earth's energy balance and producing warming.

2. Has Global Temperature Increased?

The next question was whether the observational temperature record shows a corresponding long-term change in Earth's average surface temperature.

NASA GISTEMP data show a clear increase in global temperature over the period analyzed. Relative to conditions near the beginning of the record, Earth's average surface temperature has increased by approximately 1.2°C.

The time series also illustrates an important distinction between short-term variability and long-term climate change. Individual years move above and below the surrounding trend, but the longer record shows persistent warming, with the strongest increases occurring during recent decades.

3. Has Global Sea Level Also Changed?

Because warming affects both ocean temperature and land-based ice, the next analysis examined the long-term global sea-level record.

The combined CSIRO and NOAA record shows approximately 246 millimeters — nearly 10 inches — of global sea-level rise since 1880.

Global sea level rises primarily through two processes associated with warming: seawater expands as it warms, and water previously stored in land-based glaciers and ice sheets enters the ocean as those ice masses lose mass.

Unlike short-term fluctuations in weather or local water level, global mean sea-level change represents a long-term shift in the volume and physical state of the world's oceans.

Where Is Some of That Additional Water Coming From?

The long-term records establish three major changes occurring during the modern period: rapidly increasing carbon dioxide emissions, rising global temperature, and rising global sea level.

The next step was to examine one specific physical pathway connecting warming to higher oceans: the loss of land-based ice from Greenland and Antarctica.

Unlike floating sea ice, loss of land-based ice adds water that was previously stored on land directly to the ocean. Greenland and Antarctica therefore represent two of the most important components of long-term sea-level change.

4. Are Greenland and Antarctica Losing More Ice?

IMBIE satellite-era estimates were used to calculate annualized ice loss separately for Greenland and Antarctica between 1992 and 2020. Positive values in the figure represent net annual ice loss.

Both ice sheets experienced substantial year-to-year variability, meaning that ice loss does not increase smoothly every year. However, the longer-term pattern shows that losses became much greater during the later portion of the record than during much of the early 1990s.

The estimated increase in annual ice loss was approximately 10.7 gigatonnes per year² for Greenland and 4.3 gigatonnes per year² for Antarctica over the analyzed period.

5. How Much Sea-Level Rise Came From These Ice Sheets?

Ice-mass loss can also be expressed as its equivalent contribution to global mean sea level. Combining Greenland and Antarctica makes it possible to estimate how much measurable water was added to the global ocean by the two major ice sheets.

Between 1992 and 2020, the combined losses from Greenland and Antarctica contributed approximately 21 millimeters of cumulative global sea-level rise.

Annual contributions varied substantially, but the cumulative effect continued to grow as additional land-based ice was transferred to the ocean.

Results

Taken together, the datasets show a connected sequence of changes occurring across the modern climate system.

CO₂ Emissions Increased Dramatically

Global carbon dioxide emissions increased by roughly 34,300 million tonnes from 1880 to the modern portion of the analyzed record, with particularly rapid growth occurring after the mid-twentieth century.

 

Global Temperature Increased

The global temperature record shows approximately 1.2°C of warming since 1880, with the strongest warming concentrated in recent decades despite substantial year-to-year variability.

 

Global Sea Level Rose

The global sea-level record shows approximately 246 mm — nearly 10 inches — of rise since 1880, reflecting the combined influence of ocean thermal expansion and the addition of water from melting land-based ice.

 

Ice-Sheet Loss Increased

Satellite-era observations show that both Greenland and Antarctica experienced substantial net ice loss. Estimated annual loss increased through the record by approximately 10.7 Gt/yr² in Greenland and 4.3 Gt/yr² in Antarctica.

 

Ice Loss Is Already Raising the Ocean

Greenland and Antarctica together contributed approximately 21 mm of global mean sea-level rise between 1992 and 2020, demonstrating a measurable connection between polar land-ice loss and rising oceans.

 

Conclusion

No single graph represents the entire climate system. The strength of this analysis comes from examining several independent observational records together.

The results show large increases in anthropogenic carbon dioxide emissions, a long-term increase in global temperature, continuing global sea-level rise, and substantial losses from the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets.

The ice-sheet analysis also provides a direct physical link between changes occurring in the polar regions and impacts elsewhere: land-based ice lost from Greenland and Antarctica becomes additional ocean water and contributes measurably to global sea-level rise.
